The hardest part of buying RFP software is not comparing products. It is finding out which products exist without being funnelled straight into somebody's sales pipeline.

Search for "best RFP software" and you get lists. Most are affiliate content or paid placement, several are written by vendors about their own category, and almost all describe the same six to eight products in the same order they appear in the writer's referral agreement. That is not useless information — it tells you who is spending on marketing — but it is not research.

This guide covers where candidates actually come from, how to read each source's incentives, and how to get from a long list to three serious finalists without sitting through a dozen discovery calls.

## First, segment the market you are shopping in

Before searching, decide which of four adjacent categories you actually need. They overlap enough to appear in the same search results and differ enough that buying across the boundary wastes an evaluation cycle.

**Response-side RFP software.** For teams answering inbound RFPs, RFIs and security questionnaires. Optimised around a reusable content library, question intake and assignment, and multi-contributor workflow. This is what most people mean by "RFP software" and what most of our guides address.

**Procurement-side sourcing software.** For teams *issuing* solicitations and evaluating incoming bids. Optimised around supplier discovery, solicitation distribution, bid comparison and award documentation. Often part of a broader source-to-pay suite. A response team that buys one of these has bought a mirror image of what they need.

**Proposal and document tools.** Optimised for producing designed, persuasive documents — templates, layout, pricing tables, e-signature, engagement tracking. Strong at output, usually weaker at large-library question answering.

**Adjacent specialists.** Security questionnaire automation, which is response-side work with an extreme volume-and-precision profile; and general knowledge bases, which handle storage and search well but lack the response workflow layer.

Most confused evaluations trace back to a long list that mixes two of these. Write down which one you are buying, in a sentence, and use it to disqualify quickly.

## Where good candidates actually come from

Ranked by signal quality rather than convenience.

### 1. Practitioners who do your job

The highest-value source, and the one buyers underuse because it takes a few days rather than a few minutes.

Professional communities where proposal and bid people talk to each other produce candid tooling discussion: APMP chapters and their events, sector-specific procurement associations, and the private Slack and LinkedIn groups where response teams compare notes. What you get here is unavailable anywhere else — what implementation was actually like, which promised features underdelivered, whether support answers on a deadline day.

Ask specific questions rather than "what should we buy." Good ones:

- What did you use before, and what specifically made you switch?
- What do you still do outside the tool that you expected it to handle?
- How long until your subject-matter experts stopped resisting it?
- If you were buying again tomorrow, what would you test that you did not test last time?

That last question surfaces more useful information than any feature comparison.

### 2. People who left the vendor's customers

Slightly awkward and unusually informative. Proposal professionals move between companies and carry tooling experience with them. Someone who used a product for two years at a previous employer and then chose differently at their next one has a considered opinion about a specific product's limits, formed under real conditions.

You will find these people through the same communities, or through your own network's second degree. A fifteen-minute call is worth more than a week of reading marketing sites.

### 3. Analyst and independent research

Analyst coverage of this category is thinner than for larger software markets, and worth understanding structurally before you rely on it.

Major analyst quadrants and waves cover procurement suites well and response-side RFP tools only patchily, often folding them into broader sales-enablement or content-management categories. Vendors pay for reprint rights and advisory relationships, which does not make placement purchasable in the direct sense, but does mean coverage skews toward companies large enough to engage analysts. Small, excellent, specialised products are systematically underrepresented.

Read analyst material for market structure — who is consolidating, which capabilities are becoming table stakes — rather than as a shortlist. The vendor-inclusion criteria section is often the most useful page, because it tells you who was excluded and why.

### 4. Review platforms, read correctly

G2, Capterra, TrustRadius and similar sites are genuinely useful if you read them against the grain.

Do not use the rankings. Position is influenced by review volume, recency and paid tiers, and much of the review corpus is collected through vendor campaigns that offer incentives — which biases both volume and sentiment toward recently onboarded, happy customers.

Do use them for three things. Filter to reviewers at your company size and in your industry, since a 12-person team's experience predicts little about a 300-person deployment. Read three-star reviews specifically; one- and five-star reviews are mostly emotional, while three-star reviews contain the most balanced and specific criticism on these platforms. And mine the complaints for demo questions — if four reviewers mention that importing from Excel mangles formatting, that is now a scripted task in your [demo script](/guides/how-to-buy-rfp-software).

### 5. Directories and "top 10" lists

Treat these as a census, not a ranking. Their value is completeness of the vendor universe, including products you had not heard of. Their ordering is usually commercial.

Two quick tests to read a list's incentives. Check whether outbound links carry referral parameters. And check whether every product is described positively — a list where nothing has a drawback is a marketing asset, and its ordering carries no information.

Vendor-published comparison pages ("X vs Y") deserve a similar reading. They are useful for one thing: identifying which competitors a vendor considers threatening, and which capability differences they think favour them. Both are real information, delivered with an obvious bias you can correct for.

### 6. Conference floors and category events

Efficient for parallel discovery if you go with a plan. Bid and proposal conferences put most of the category in one room, and a fifteen-minute booth conversation with a prepared question list eliminates candidates faster than a scheduled call.

Go with three disqualifying questions — the ones where a wrong answer ends the conversation. Something like: does content ownership and review scheduling exist at the item level; can a subject-matter expert answer without a licensed seat; is your data residency configurable. You will clear a long list in an afternoon.

### 7. Your own inbound RFPs, and your competitors

Two overlooked sources. First, if you receive RFPs from procurement teams, the portals and formats you deal with tell you which procurement-side systems are common in your market — which matters for integration and export requirements.

Second, job postings. A competitor hiring a proposal manager and naming a specific platform in the requirements has told you what they use. Aggregated across a dozen postings in your sector, this is a reasonable map of what is actually deployed rather than what is marketed.

## Building the long list

Aim for eight to fifteen names. Capture, for each, only what you can find without talking to anyone:

| Field | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Product and company name | Distinguish products from suites they sit inside |
| Segment | Response-side, procurement-side, proposal, adjacent |
| Apparent target size | SMB, mid-market, enterprise — from their own case studies |
| Published pricing | Whether they publish at all is itself a signal about sales model |
| Certifications listed | SOC 2, ISO 27001 — a fast must-have filter |
| Ownership | Independent, PE-backed, part of a larger suite |
| Source | Where you found them, so you can weigh the source's bias |

Two hours of desk research fills this in. Resist the urge to book calls while doing it — every call costs you an hour and creates a relationship you may have to end.

## Screening without discovery calls

Here is the step that saves the most time in the whole process: screen with a written questionnaire instead of introductory calls.

Send eight to twelve questions by email to your long list, drawn from your must-haves, and ask for written answers within a week. Say explicitly that you are not scheduling calls at this stage. A workable set:

1. Can a subject-matter expert answer an assigned question without a paid seat? If yes, how?
2. Can each library item carry a named owner and a review date, with a report of overdue items?
3. What happens to submitted responses when a library answer they used is later edited?
4. Which import formats do you support for question intake, and what breaks most often?
5. Is your SOC 2 Type II report available under NDA, and what period does it cover?
6. Where is customer data hosted, and is the region configurable?
7. Is customer content used to train models available to other customers? Can we contractually opt out?
8. What is included in standard implementation, in hours and named deliverables?
9. What is your list price at 25 seats, and what is the expected renewal uplift?
10. Which of the above are on your roadmap rather than available today?

This does three things at once. It filters on must-haves before anyone spends an hour on a call. It gives you written answers you can put directly into the vendor-response column of your [compliance matrix](/guides/compliance-matrix-to-select-rfp-software). And it tests something no demo tests: how clearly and directly a vendor answers a specific question in writing. Evasive or generic written answers are a reliable predictor of the same during implementation.

Expect some vendors to refuse and insist on a call first. That is a data point about how they will treat you as a customer, and in my experience it correlates with a sales-led motion where information is deliberately withheld until a relationship exists. It is not automatically disqualifying, but it earns a note.

## Choosing finalists

From the screening responses, pick three to five for full demos. Deliberately include:

- **The two strongest fits** on your weighted must-haves.
- **One product from a different segment or size tier** than the others — a smaller specialist against two established players, or a suite module against standalone tools. This is a cheap hedge against having framed the problem too narrowly, and it occasionally wins.

Do not include a product you have already decided against as a courtesy or to make a comparison look thorough. It wastes their time and yours, and a padded field makes the eventual recommendation easier to challenge, not harder.

Tell the vendors you rejected why, briefly and specifically. It costs you five minutes, and in a small category with mobile practitioners, having a reputation for running a clean process is worth more than the awkwardness saved.

## Verifying the shortlist

Two checks before you commit, both of which change decisions more often than buyers expect.

**Reference calls with a script.** Ask each finalist for two customers at your size and in your industry, plus — ask anyway — one that downgraded or churned. Prepare questions that cannot be answered with a testimonial: What did implementation actually take, versus the estimate? What do you still do outside the tool? What was the worst week you had with it? How long until SMEs stopped resisting? What would you negotiate differently?

**Vendor viability.** Check funding history and date of last raise, headcount trend, release note cadence over the past twelve months, and whether the product has been folded into a larger suite. Consolidation in this category has been steady, and a product in maintenance mode is not disqualifying if it fits today — but it should change how much you weight roadmap promises and how hard you push on export rights, term length and renewal caps.

## What good vendor research looks like when it is done

You should end with three to five named finalists, a written record of why each of the others was eliminated, screening answers in hand for your matrix, and at least two reference conversations with people who have no stake in your decision.

That is a week of work, most of it asynchronous. It is also the difference between choosing from the products that market to you and choosing from the products that exist.
